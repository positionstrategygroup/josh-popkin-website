The dot.LA Story About Carter Agency Was Built on One Interview

September 17, 2026 · ← Back to Insights

Josh Popkin in New York, 2026.

The article that has ranked first for Josh Popkin’s name since December 2022 was written by a dot.LA editorial fellow, quotes one person, cites no document the reporter reviewed, and has never been updated. A close read of the piece, and of roughly 28 others by the same writer, shows how it was built. Every claim below can be checked against the article itself.

Who Wrote the dot.LA Article About Carter Agency?

The article was written by Kristin Snyder, who was dot.LA’s 2022/23 Editorial Fellow, a one-year post held after leading the arts section of UCLA’s student paper and an internship at a regional magazine publisher.

Her byline at dot.LA runs from May 2022 to June 2023. The Carter Agency piece was published on December 1, 2022, about seven months in. Most of her other work that year is about TikTok, the creator economy, and startup funding. The Carter Agency piece is the only one about a named individual said to have done something wrong, and it is the only one that has stayed on the first page of Google for anyone’s name.

That is the part worth sitting with. A fellowship-year article, written on a small venture-backed site that covers Los Angeles tech, has outranked everything else about Josh Popkin since 2022. Not because it was thorough. Because nothing else was there.

How Many Sources Did the dot.LA Carter Agency Article Have?

One person is quoted directly in the article: the influencer who brought the story. A second person, the co-founder of an industry group, is paraphrased for general context about agency scams. No one else speaks.

No brand is quoted. No second creator is quoted. No lawyer is quoted. No investigator is quoted. The article says a civil suit and a criminal investigation were underway. Neither is identified by court, case number, agency, or date, and nobody connected to either one is heard from.

For a piece whose headline says a company scammed “hundreds” of people, one interview is not reporting. It is transcription.

What Evidence Did the Reporter Review Herself?

The article does not say the reporter reviewed a single document.

No screenshots of the messages it describes. No emails. No contracts. No court filing. No police report. No brand confirmation of what was offered versus what was passed along. The only independent record cited anywhere in the piece is a corporate registry listing, which established that Carter Agency LLC existed and who registered it.

Everything else, every dollar figure and every description of how the scheme supposedly worked, is one person’s account, retold.

Where Did the “Hundreds of Influencers” Number Come From?

The headline says “hundreds.” The body says “more than 130.” The 130 is attributed to the same single source, described in the article’s own words as something she “claims.”

So the number went through three stages inside one article. A claim by the person with the grievance. A body sentence that turned the claim into a count. A headline that rounded the count up to a word the body never supports.

The $60,000 figure is the same. It is her estimate of deals she believes were affected, and it appears in the article as her claim. Nine months later, another outlet, Passionfruit, cited the dot.LA piece as the source for a creator who “claimed a talent agent stole $60,000 from her.” That is how a single unverified number becomes the record. One outlet prints it, the next one links to the first, and after that nobody asks where it started.

Does the Article Say “Allegedly”?

Once. In the headline.

After that, the hedging thins out and the claims drift into the writer’s own voice. The article states as fact that the agency’s actions “have affected more than 130 influencers, including those signed to Popkin’s company and those who he falsely claimed to represent.” No “allegedly.” No “according to.” That sentence is the reporter asserting, in her own words, something she had one person’s word for.

This is the kind of thing a copy desk exists to catch. dot.LA did not catch it, and it has been live for almost four years.

Did dot.LA Contact Josh Popkin for Comment?

The article says the agency “has not responded to multiple requests for comment.” That is the entire account of the outreach.

It does not say when the requests were sent. It does not say where, whether to a company inbox, a personal email, a phone number, or a social account. It does not say how long the reporter waited before publishing. It does not say whether Popkin was contacted as a person, as opposed to the agency as a company.

Standard practice at a serious outlet is to name the channel and the deadline, so a reader can judge whether the subject really had a chance. One sentence with no details tells the reader nothing. It only protects the writer.

Why Does the Article Mention a 2020 Cereal Video?

Because it makes the subject look bad, and for no other reason.

The article notes that Popkin faced backlash in 2020 over a viral prank video, and then lets the source speculate that he used a different first name to distance himself from it. A prank video has nothing to do with how brand deals were priced in 2022. It is in the article for character, not for evidence. It tells the reader what to think about him before they have been shown anything.

Was the dot.LA Carter Agency Article Ever Updated?

No.

The article reports that a civil suit and a criminal investigation were in progress. Kristin Snyder never published a follow-up on either one. dot.LA never published one. There is no editor’s note, no correction, no update line. The byline ends in June 2023 and the story stayed frozen at the claim stage, ranking, with no outcome attached.

When an outlet publishes that a person is under criminal investigation, the outlet owes its readers the ending. Four years is long enough to have found one.

Was This a One-Off, or a Pattern in Her Reporting?

It was a pattern. Reading the rest of the year makes that plain.

  • Low source counts. About half of the roughly 28 articles reviewed rest on one or two named sources. Company profiles on Whalar, Superplastic, Creatorland, and Ubiquitous quote only that company’s own executive.
  • Opposing views are rare. Roughly four of the 28 pieces include any counter-perspective at all.
  • Rewritten announcements. Pieces on TikTok’s Branded Mission program, the VMAs’ metaverse category, and the YouTube-versus-TikTok monetization race lean on company statements and on Business Insider and TechCrunch coverage rather than original interviews.
  • Social posts as sourcing. TikTok videos and social reactions stand in for evidence in several pieces, including the Carter Agency article and a recap of the TikTok congressional hearing.
  • Unsourced statistics. Figures like an “over $100 billion industry” and “over one billion views” appear with no origin.
  • Softer on companies than on people. The venture firms and startups she profiled got flattering, single-source features. The one individual she wrote about got a headline with “scammed” in it, on the strength of the same single-source method.

The method did not change between the Whalar profile and the Carter Agency piece. What changed was who it was aimed at.

What Is a Fair Critique, and What Is Not?

The fair critique is about method. One source. No documents. Claims drifting into the writer’s voice. A headline the body does not support. No follow-up on the legal outcomes the piece itself raised. Those are checkable, and they are laid out above so anyone can check them.

What would not be fair is to call the writer dishonest or to suggest she set out to hurt anyone. Nothing here says that. She was in her first year, at a small outlet, doing the fast version of a creator-economy story. Editors are supposed to slow that down. Nobody did.

What Did Carter Agency Actually Do?

Carter Agency was a creator-management and influencer-marketing agency founded by Josh Popkin and his brother, Ben Popkin. It represented TikTok creators, many with over a million followers, and connected them to brand campaigns for companies including Cap’n Crunch, Nissin Foods, Samsung, and HP. Some of that work is written up at joshpopkincarteragency.com, with the brand, the year, and what the videos looked like, and in the Carter Agency FAQ on this site.

None of that is in the dot.LA article, and none of it could be, because nobody asked.

Source: Kristin Snyder, “PR Firm Carter Agency Allegedly Scammed Hundreds of Influencers Out of Brand Deals,” dot.LA, December 1, 2022. All quotations above are from that article as it appeared in September 2026.
